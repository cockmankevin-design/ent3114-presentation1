02 — Problem Statement

Buyers can't tell which peptide sources they can trust — and don't know a legal, approved alternative even exists.

Someone looking into buying peptides online today has to piece together Google, Reddit, YouTube and social media, and still isn't sure. The certificates of analysis unregulated vendors publish can't be verified — some name a lab that never issued the document, some reuse one file across an entire catalogue — so the one document buyers were taught to trust is the cheapest thing in the market to fake.

Nobody in that search result is pointing them to the fact that FDA-approved versions of several popular peptides already exist, obtainable through a doctor, manufacturer direct-pay, or licensed telehealth.

02 / 13

03 — Customer Persona

Two segments, not one — and they're not the same buyer.

Gym / peptide users

Current grey-market buyers of compounds like BPC-157 and TB-500. Most of what they actually want has no FDA-approved version yet — the hardest segment to convert to a legal route.

General-health people

Closer to the GLP-1 and telehealth buyer PeptideChecker now actually serves. More likely to accept a licensed, approved alternative if the cost gap is smaller than they assume.

One real interview completed so far. Course requirement is ten — five gym/peptide users and four general-health people, compared against each other, are still owed.

03 / 13

04 — Hypothesis

We believe buyers will choose the legal route — if the price gap is smaller than they assume, and the guide proves its own honesty.

Specifically: people who buy unverified research peptides today will switch to an FDA-approved telehealth route once they see it priced against what they're already paying, and once PeptideChecker demonstrates — openly, on its own methodology page — exactly how it checks vendors and that no provider currently pays it a commission.

This is the leap-of-faith bet. It fails if buyers already know the real price gap and choose cheaper anyway — which is exactly what the one interview completed so far suggests might happen.

05 — Test Results

What the interview actually said

"I usually have to look through a bunch of different websites and reviews."
"I'd definitely use something like that — right now I have to piece all of that information together myself."
"I wouldn't want it to just be a list where companies pay to be ranked higher."
"I'd rather pay a little more for something I actually trusted."

But also, the honest risk:

Asked directly whether this interviewee would take the legal telehealth route once they knew it existed, my own read was: "They'd probably go back to the vendors because it's cheaper." The trust problem is real. Whether buyers will actually pay more to solve it legally is still unproven.

05 / 13

05 — Test Results, continued

Key changes to the problem assumptions

The original plan was to score and publicly rank grey-market vendors' own certificates of analysis, earning affiliate commission from the vendors that passed. Research into the legal and credibility risk of that model drove a pivot:

Before

Verify and rank grey-market research-peptide vendors. Earn commission from the vendors being scored.

Now

Point buyers to FDA-approved medicines and licensed telehealth instead. Keep a small evidence section explaining why unregulated sellers fail basic checks — no ranking of them, no revenue from them.

The underlying trust problem didn't change. The recommended fix did.

08 — How It Works

Every date and number comes from the FDA's own records.

Approved & pipeline directory

Generic name, approval date, and FDA application number pulled directly from Drugs@FDA for every entry — every row says when it was last checked.

Vendor evidence section

A seven-point check applied to unregulated vendors' own published certificates: loads, names the lab, the lab confirms it, issued to this vendor, names a lot, matches the page, tested within six months.

No overall score is published for either side — a status plus the checks, shown openly, is the entire product.

11 — Cost Structure

Near-zero cash cost. The real cost is time.

Near zero

Hosting on GitHub Pages' free tier, static export, no server, no database beyond what the site itself needs.

The real recurring cost

Founder time: keeping FDA data current, running the nine remaining customer interviews, and — once a telehealth program accepts — the ongoing compliance work that comes with it.

No lab, no inventory, no payroll, deliberately.

12 — Critical Factors

Three things that could end this before it starts.

Revenue

No affiliate program has accepted PeptideChecker

The entire revenue plan depends on at least one telehealth provider saying yes. Zero have so far.

Behavior

Will buyers actually switch?

The one real interview suggests price, not trust, may still win. The next nine interviews are the real test of the hypothesis on slide four.

Proof

No first-party data yet

VialAudit can point to 1,816 real certificates it analyzed. PeptideChecker has nothing equivalent published yet.
